🧠 The Mindset Shift

From "I ship code" to "I ship people who ship code" — yes, you're a meta-shipper now

"As a Lead SE, your PR was code. As an EM, your PR is people's careers.
And unlike code, people don't have a --force flag."

❌ Lead Software Engineer

  • My code runs in production
  • I architect systems
  • I review PRs at 11 PM
  • I mentor through code
  • My IDE is open 8+ hours
  • Bugs keep me up at night

✅ Engineering Manager

  • My team's code runs in production

Your success is no longer measured by YOUR output — it's measured by your TEAM's output multiplied by their happiness coefficient. Yes, that's a real thing. No, you can't unit test it.

👥 People Management

Congratulations! You now manage humans. They're like microservices but with feelings.

"Managing a .NET application is easy — it either compiles or it doesn't. Managing people? They compile fine but throw runtime exceptions you NEVER expected."
🗣️ 1:1 Meetings — Your Most Important Tool ▼

Frequency: Weekly, 30 mins minimum. Never cancel. NEVER.

The GROW Framework for 1:1s:

  • Goal — What do they want to achieve?
  • Reality — Where are they now?
  • Options — What paths exist?
  • Will — What will they commit to?
🎯 Power Questions to Ask
  • "What's the one thing I could do to make your job easier?"
  • "If you could change one thing about our team, what would it be?"
  • "What's something you're proud of this week that nobody noticed?"
  • "Where do you see yourself in 12 months? How can I help?"
⚠️ Anti-Pattern Alert
Don't turn 1:1s into status updates. You have Jira for that (unfortunately).
📊 Performance Management & Feedback ▼

The SBI Feedback Model (Situation-Behavior-Impact):

Instead of: "Your code reviews are bad"

Say: "In yesterday's PR review (Situation), I noticed you approved without comments on the missing null checks (Behavior). This caused a NullReferenceException in production that took the team 2 hours to fix (Impact)."

Performance Rating Framework:

  • 🚀 Exceeding: Consistently delivers beyond expectations, uplevels others
  • ✅ Meeting: Reliably delivers at level, growing steadily
  • ⚠️ Developing: Has gaps, needs coaching, on a growth plan
  • 🔴 Below: On PIP. This is where your HR partnership skills matter
🎯 Remember
Feedback should never be a surprise during review time. If it is, YOU failed as a manager, not them.
🔥 Conflict Resolution ▼

The DESC Method:

  • Describe the situation objectively
  • Express your concerns
  • Suggest alternatives
  • Consequences — state what happens if unresolved

Scenario: Two senior devs fighting over whether to use MediatR or not.

Bad approach: "Just use what I say, I'm the manager."

Good approach: "Let's timebox this decision. Each of you prepare a 10-minute ADR (Architecture Decision Record) with pros/cons. We'll evaluate against our team's specific constraints: throughput needs, team familiarity, and maintenance overhead. We decide by Thursday."

💡 Conflict is Healthy
Teams without conflict are teams where people don't feel safe enough to disagree. Your job is to make conflict productive, not eliminate it.

📦 Delivery & Execution

Because at the end of the day, shipping matters. But HOW you ship matters more.

"A Lead SE worries about whether the code compiles. An EM worries about whether the quarter compiles."

📅 Sprint/Iteration Management

  • Velocity is a PLANNING tool, not a PERFORMANCE tool
  • Protect the sprint. Say "no" with data.
  • Retrospectives are sacred — fix ONE thing per retro
  • If your standup exceeds 15 min, you have too many people or too little structure
⚠️ Never Do This
Never use velocity to compare teams or pressure individuals. That's how you get story point inflation — the developer equivalent of money printing.

📊 Stakeholder Communication

  • Executive Level: Outcomes, not outputs. "Revenue impact" not "story points"
  • Product Level: Trade-offs, timelines, risks
  • Team Level: Context, priorities, protection from noise
💡 The Umbrella Principle
You are an umbrella. Shit flows down from leadership. Your job is to convert it into actionable, calm context before it reaches your team.

🚨 Incident Management

  • Build an on-call rotation that doesn't burn people out
  • Blameless post-mortems (ALWAYS)
  • Incident Commander role — train your team
  • SLOs > SLAs. Error budgets are your friend.

🗺️ Roadmap & Planning

  • Now/Next/Later > Exact Gantt dates
  • Dual-track agile: Discovery + Delivery
  • Capacity planning: Account for 20% unplanned work
  • Dependencies are risks. Map them. Mitigate them.

🤝 Stakeholder Management & Communication

Because code compiles in milliseconds but alignment takes calendar slots and slide decks

"A stakeholder is anyone who can ask for 'just one more thing' right before a release. Treat them like a dependency — manage them early or they'll block your deploy."
📣 Communicating Up — Speaking Executive ▼

The BLUF Framework (Bottom Line Up Front):

Bad: "So we started investigating the authentication service and found some issues with token refresh that could potentially impact users under certain conditions if we don't address it..."

Good: "Our auth service has a token refresh bug that affects ~2% of mobile users. We've scoped the fix to 3 days. We need a decision: patch now with a minor UX change, or wait 2 weeks for the clean fix. I recommend patching now."

The Executive Communication Checklist:

  • Lead with the decision needed or impact, never with context
  • Quantify everything: time, cost, users affected, revenue risk
  • Bring options (2-3 max), your recommendation, and the trade-offs
  • Anticipate questions: "What's the risk if we do nothing?" "How confident are you?"
  • Follow up in writing after verbal conversations — memory degrades
⚠️ Common EM Communication Mistakes
  • Burying the ask at the end of a long email
  • Over-explaining implementation details to non-technical stakeholders
  • Saying "it's complicated" — that's not an answer, it's a surrender
  • Waiting until the deadline to escalate a problem

How to Say No to Stakeholders (with Data):

  • Never say "we can't do that" — say "here's what it would cost to do that vs. what we'd deprioritize"
  • Make trade-offs explicit: "We can do X by Friday OR Y by next Thursday — not both"
  • Use the capacity equation: show your team's velocity and current commitments
  • Offer alternatives: "We can't do the full feature, but here's an 80% solution in half the time"
💡 The Stakeholder Trust Bank
Every time you deliver on a commitment, you make a deposit. Every time you miss without early warning, you make a withdrawal. Run your stakeholder relationships like a bank account — stay in surplus.

⚠️ Goodhart's Law Warning
"When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." If you track story points too hard, your team will inflate estimates. Use metrics to spot trends, not to score individuals.

Metrics That Actually Signal Team Health:

  • PR review turnaround time (proxy for collaboration speed)
  • Bug escape rate to production (proxy for quality culture)
  • Unplanned work % per sprint (proxy for technical debt pressure)
  • Team satisfaction score (run quarterly pulse surveys — eNPS)

🏛️ Engineering Culture & AI in .NET

Culture eats strategy for breakfast — and AI is changing what's on the menu

"Your team won't remember the sprints you shipped. They'll remember how you made them feel when the production was down at 2 AM and you were calm, clear, and blameless."
🧱 Building Psychological Safety ▼

Amy Edmondson's 4-Stage Safety Ladder:

StageTeam BehaviorEM Signal
1. Inclusion SafetyFeel welcome on the teamAll voices heard in retro
2. Learner SafetySafe to ask questions & failNo punishment for mistakes
3. Contributor SafetySafe to add value in own wayEngineers propose solutions
4. Challenger SafetySafe to challenge the status quoTeam pushes back on bad ideas

Daily EM Practices That Build Safety:

  • Model vulnerability: admit your own mistakes publicly
  • Blameless post-mortems: "What failed in the system?" not "Who failed?"
  • Celebrate "good failures" — fast experiments that revealed something valuable
  • Follow through on every concern raised — even if the answer is "not now, because..."
⚠️ Safety Destroyers to Avoid
  • Interrupting or dismissing ideas in meetings
  • Rewarding a "brilliant jerk" who makes others feel stupid
  • Shoot-the-messenger responses to bad news
  • Inconsistent standards — calling out some but not others

📐 Engineering Norms & Working Agreements ▼

What Every .NET Team's Working Agreement Should Cover:

  • Code style: .editorconfig checked into source control — no debates, just compliance
  • PR size: <400 lines; larger PRs need a conversation first
  • Review SLA: First response within 4 hours, merge-ready within 24 hours
  • On-call rotation: Published, fair, and with documented runbooks
  • Definition of Done: Tests pass, monitoring in place, feature flag ready, docs updated
  • Communication defaults: Slack for async, huddles only for blockers, email for formal decisions
💡 The Working Agreement Ritual
Review your working agreement at every team retro. If the team isn't following a norm, either enforce it or remove it — dead rules erode trust in all rules.

🎤 Interview Questions & How to Answer

The questions that will decide if you get the title (and the meetings that come with it)

"Interviewing for EM is weird. They ask you behavioral questions and you have to pretend you've never rage-quit a code review."
🌟 The STAR Method — Your Best Friend

Situation → Task → Action → Result

Every behavioral answer should follow this. Add Learning at the end for bonus points (STARL).

❓ "Tell me about a time you had to deliver difficult feedback" ▼
🔥 COMMON — Asked 90% of the time

STAR Answer:

Situation: "On my team of 6 .NET developers, a senior engineer was consistently submitting PRs with minimal test coverage, averaging 15% when our standard was 80%."

Task: "I needed to address this without damaging our relationship or their confidence, as they were otherwise a strong contributor."

Action: "I scheduled a private 1:1, started by acknowledging their strong domain knowledge, then used the SBI model: 'In the last 3 PRs, test coverage was below 20%. This caused 2 production bugs that required hotfixes, impacting the team's deployment confidence.' I asked open-ended questions to understand WHY — turned out they felt pressured by deadlines and saw tests as 'nice to have.' We co-created a plan: I adjusted sprint capacity expectations, paired them with our testing champion, and set a graduated target (50% → 65% → 80% over 6 weeks)."

Result: "Within 8 weeks, their coverage was consistently above 80%, they became an advocate for testing, and our team's change failure rate dropped by 40%."

Learning: "I learned that resistance to practices often signals resource constraints, not attitude problems."

❌ Never Say This
  • "I told them to do better or they'd be fired" (authoritarian)
  • "I didn't really address it, they eventually improved" (avoidant)
  • "I just did the tests myself" (martyr — doesn't scale)
❓ "How would you handle a team member who wants to become a manager?" ▼
🔥 COMMON

Great Answer Framework:

"First, I'd explore their motivation. Do they want management because they genuinely want to grow people, or because they think it's the only way to advance? Many orgs have dual-track ladders (IC Staff/Principal track)."

"If they're genuinely interested, I'd create a 'management rotation' opportunity:

  • Lead sprint ceremonies for a month
  • Own a 1:1 with a junior developer (with my oversight)
  • Represent the team in cross-team planning
  • Handle a small hiring loop

"After 2-3 months of exposure, we'd have an honest conversation: Did they enjoy it? Did they miss coding? Do they understand the trade-offs? Then I'd either champion their promotion or help them find fulfillment in the IC track."

❓ "How do you balance technical debt with feature delivery?" ▼
🔥🔥 VERY COMMON — .NET specific angle helps

Strong Answer:

"I use a three-pronged approach:

1. Make it visible: We maintain a tech debt backlog with business impact scores. Every item has a 'cost of delay' estimate. For example, 'Our EF Core queries have N+1 problems — if we don't fix them before Black Friday, page load times will exceed 3s, costing ~$50K in lost conversions.'

2. Budget it: 20% of each sprint is reserved for tech health. Non-negotiable. I frame it to stakeholders as 'investment in deployment speed' — because tech debt directly impacts DORA metrics.

3. Boy Scout Rule: Every PR should leave the codebase slightly better. Refactoring isn't a separate task — it's embedded in how we work. If you touch a file, you can spend 30 minutes improving it.

"In my last role, this approach helped us migrate from .NET Framework 4.8 to .NET 8 incrementally over 6 months while STILL delivering features every sprint."

❓ "Describe a time you had to make a decision with incomplete information" ▼
🔥🔥 Tests Decision-Making

STAR Answer:

Situation: "We were 2 weeks from a major release when our Azure SQL database started showing intermittent deadlocks under load. We had to decide: delay the release, throw hardware at it, or ship with a risk."

Task: "I needed to make a call within 24 hours as the team lead, balancing business commitment with production stability."

Action: "I gathered what we knew: the deadlocks occurred only above 500 concurrent users (our peak was 350), and only on one specific write-heavy endpoint. I applied the 'reversibility' framework: Is this decision easily reversible? Yes — we could hotfix or feature-flag. I decided to ship with: (1) a feature flag on the risky endpoint, (2) enhanced monitoring with alerts at 400 users, and (3) a pre-written rollback runbook. I communicated the risk and mitigation to stakeholders transparently."

Result: "We shipped on time. The deadlock never materialized in production (our load modeling was conservative). The team then fixed the root cause in the following sprint without pressure."

Learning: "I learned the framework: 'What's the cost of being wrong? How quickly can we reverse? What monitoring do we need?' — this has guided every ambiguous decision since."

❓ "How would you handle two senior engineers who can't work together?" ▼
🔥🔥 Conflict Resolution

Framework Answer:

"First, I'd have individual conversations with each to understand their perspective without judgment. Often conflict comes from misaligned expectations, not personal animosity."

"Common root causes I'd investigate:

  • Unclear ownership boundaries (both think they own the architecture)
  • Different quality standards (one wants 'done right', other wants 'done fast')
  • Communication style mismatch (one is blunt, other is sensitive)
  • Career competition (both want the same promotion)

"Then I'd facilitate a structured conversation: acknowledge both perspectives, identify shared goals, and create clear agreements. For example, if it's about architecture ownership, I'd formalize a RACI matrix."

"If the conflict persists after good-faith intervention, I'd consider structural solutions: different projects, different pods, or — as a last resort — acknowledging that one might need to move teams."

"I never ignore conflict. Unresolved tension is a team cancer that metastasizes."

❓ "What's your approach to system design decisions as an EM?" ▼
⚙️ Technical Leadership

Answer:

"My role as an EM in system design is to be the 'decision catalyst', not the 'decision maker'. Here's my approach:

1. Create the process: We use lightweight RFCs/ADRs. Any change affecting more than one service requires a written proposal reviewed by 2+ engineers.

2. Ask the right questions: 'What are the operational implications? Who will be on-call for this? What's the failure mode? How do we rollback?'

3. Break deadlocks: If the team can't agree after reasonable debate, I'll make the call — but I'll explain my reasoning and commit to revisiting in 3 months.

4. Context from above: I bring information the team doesn't have — upcoming business pivots, budget constraints, hiring plans — that should influence architecture."

"For example, when choosing between Azure Service Bus and Kafka, I didn't make the technical choice. But I DID provide context: 'We're merging with Team X in Q3, and they're on Kafka. Consider interoperability.' That context changed the decision."

❓ "How do you measure engineering team success?" ▼
📊 Metrics-Oriented

Answer:

"I measure across four dimensions:

1. Delivery: DORA metrics (deployment frequency, lead time, MTTR, change failure rate). These tell me if our engineering machine is healthy.

2. Quality: Production incident rate, SLO compliance, customer-reported bugs. This tells me if we're shipping the right things well.

3. People: Engagement scores, retention rate, promotion velocity, eNPS. This tells me if the team is sustainable.

4. Business Impact: Feature adoption rates, revenue influence, time-to-market for key initiatives. This connects us to company goals.

"I visualize these on a team health dashboard reviewed monthly. The key insight: these metrics should be used for LEARNING, not JUDGING. If deployment frequency drops, I ask 'what changed?' not 'who's slacking?'"

Someone wants to leave "I appreciate you telling me. Help me understand what's driving this."

🚨 Common EM Anti-Patterns (Avoid These!)

Anti-Pattern Symptom Fix
The Hero Manager You code all night fixing bugs your team should handle Coach, don't do. Ask "who on the team could grow from this?"
The Meeting Machine 8 hours of meetings, 0 hours of thinking Audit meetings quarterly. "Does this need me? Could it be async?"
The Conflict Avoider Toxic behavior festers, good people leave Address issues within 48 hours. Discomfort now prevents disaster later.
The Micromanager You review every PR, approve every decision Set guardrails, not gatekeeps. Trust but verify periodically.
The Yes-Person Team is overloaded, nothing ships well Every "yes" is a "no" to something else. Make trade-offs explicit.
The Ivory Tower You make architecture decisions without context Stay close to the work. Monthly code review, weekly design discussions.
